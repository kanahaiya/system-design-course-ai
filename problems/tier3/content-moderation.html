Tier 3 — Expert 60 minTrust & Safety

Design a Content Moderation System (like YouTube / Meta)

Content moderation is a multi-layer pipeline: hash-based detection for known-bad content (deterministic, fast, no ML), ML classifiers for novel violations, and human review for borderline cases. The key architectural tensions are proactive vs reactive (block before publish or remove after), automated vs human-reviewed, and precision vs recall trade-offs per violation type. Designing this system well requires understanding all three layers and why each exists.

🏢 YouTube, Meta, Twitter/X, TikTok
⏱️ 60 min interview
📊 500 hr video/min, 100B images/day

❓R — Requirements3–5 min

Functional Requirements

  • Multi-modal detection: detect violations in images, video, text, and audio. Different models per modality.
  • Violation categories: CSAM (child sexual abuse material), terrorism/violent extremism, graphic violence, hate speech, spam/scam, copyright infringement, misinformation. Each has different severity, legal requirements, and confidence thresholds.
  • Hash-based matching: detect exact or near-duplicate copies of previously identified illegal content (CSAM, terrorism propaganda) via perceptual hashing and PhotoDNA. Deterministic, no false positives.
  • ML classifiers: detect novel violations not in hash databases. Output confidence score (0-1). Route to human review if confidence is in uncertain range.
  • Human review queue: priority-ordered queue for cases that automated systems cannot resolve with sufficient confidence. Route to reviewer with appropriate expertise.
  • Appeals process: users can appeal moderation decisions. Appeals go to a different reviewer than the original decision.
  • Legal hold: content flagged for law enforcement investigation must be preserved even if the user deletes it. Legal hold overrides all delete operations.
  • Transparency reporting: publish statistics on content removed by category, appeals outcome rates, and false positive rates.

Non-Functional Requirements

  • Scale: YouTube: 500 hours of video uploaded per minute. Facebook: 100B+ images uploaded per day. 99.9%+ of content handled automatically.
  • CSAM detection latency: must detect and block before content is ever visible. <1 second from upload completion to hash check result.
  • False positive rate: <0.1% for automated removals (falsely removing legitimate content). Precision is favored over recall for automated removal. Recall is favored over precision for routing to human review (better to over-review than under-review).
  • Reviewer throughput: human reviewer handles 100-200 cases/day. With 0.1% of YouTube's 720K daily video uploads requiring review: 720 cases/day → 4-7 reviewers. At Facebook scale (billions of posts): thousands of reviewers.
  • Audit trail: every moderation decision (automated or human) logged immutably for legal and transparency purposes.

Out of Scope

  • Reviewer wellbeing and trauma support systems (critically important IRL, but separate from system design)
  • Copyright detection beyond hash matching (Content ID system is a separate complex system)
  • Real-time live stream moderation (additional latency constraints, covered separately)

🔢E — Estimation3–5 min

Scale Numbers

  • YouTube upload rate: 500 hours of video/minute = 8.3 hours/sec. At average 1GB/hour: 8.3GB/sec ingestion. Each video needs hash check + ML classification.
  • Image classification throughput: Facebook 100B images/day = 1.16M images/sec. A GPU can classify ~10K images/sec per A100. Need ~120 A100 GPUs for image classification alone.
  • Text classification: Facebook posts + Instagram captions + comments: ~1B text items/day = 11.6K items/sec. BERT inference on GPU: ~500 items/sec per GPU. Need ~25 GPUs for text moderation.
  • Hash database size: CSAM hash database (NCMEC PhotoDNA): ~6M known hashes. Each SHA-256 hash: 32 bytes. Total: 192MB — fits entirely in memory for fast lookup. pHash database (similar size): also fits in memory.
  • Human review queue: 0.1% automated review rate = 100K cases/day at Facebook scale. With 200 reviews/reviewer/day: need 500 reviewers globally. Actually Meta employs 15,000+ content reviewers — suggesting the 0.1% estimate is low for their scale.
💡 The human review math at scale: Even if automation handles 99.9% of cases, the remaining 0.1% at Facebook scale (billions of daily uploads) = millions of cases per day requiring human review. This is why content moderation at scale requires thousands of human reviewers despite 99.9%+ automation. This also means reviewer tooling, queue management, and reviewer wellness are non-trivial engineering problems.

Proactive vs Reactive Moderation

This is the most important architectural decision:

  • Proactive (pre-publish): content is held in queue, moderation runs synchronously, content is only published if it passes. Advantage: harmful content never reaches viewers. Disadvantage: latency (legitimate content delayed), throughput bottleneck (moderation must match upload rate), false positives block legitimate posts. Mandatory for CSAM (legal requirement), appropriate for high-risk accounts.
  • Reactive (post-publish): content published immediately, moderation runs asynchronously, violations removed after detection. Advantage: no latency for legitimate users, scales independently from upload. Disadvantage: harmful content has a visibility window between upload and removal. Acceptable for spam and minor violations. Not acceptable for CSAM or terrorism.
  • Risk-based routing: new accounts or accounts with violation history: proactive. Established accounts: reactive. Content type: videos >30 minutes: reactive (moderation takes longer than 30min). Text: reactive. Images with uploaded thumbnails that match known patterns: proactive.
🔴 Legal mandate for CSAM: The detection and reporting of CSAM (child sexual abuse material) is not optional. Under US law (NCMEC Cyber Tipline), platforms are legally required to report known CSAM to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. PhotoDNA hash matching for CSAM must be proactive (before content is published) and must result in report to NCMEC. This is a hard system requirement, not a product decision. Design must make this unbypassable.

🔬D — Deep Dive10 min

Hash-Based Detection: Exact vs Perceptual

Hash matching is the fastest and most reliable detection method for known-bad content. Two types serve different purposes:

  • Exact hash (SHA-256): a cryptographic hash of the exact file bytes. If even one pixel is changed, the hash changes completely. Use for: known CSAM files reported to NCMEC, known terrorism videos (e.g., Christchurch attack footage). Fast lookup: O(1) in a hash set. No false positives. Easily evaded by trivial modifications (one pixel change).
  • Perceptual hash (pHash): a hash that is similar for visually similar images. Based on DCT (discrete cosine transform) of a downscaled version of the image. Two images with pHash distance < threshold are "the same image." Robust to minor modifications (JPEG re-compression, slight cropping, color adjustment). Can have false positives (structurally similar but unrelated images). Used for: NSFW images, spam image reuse.
  • PhotoDNA (Microsoft): hash specifically designed for CSAM detection by Microsoft, donated to NCMEC. More robust than pHash, tuned for human body image similarity. Industry standard for CSAM detection at platforms. Not open source (proprietary, licensed to platforms through NCMEC).
// pHash algorithm sketch:
// 1. Resize image to 32×32 pixels
// 2. Convert to grayscale
// 3. Apply DCT (discrete cosine transform) to 32×32 → 32×32 frequency matrix
// 4. Take top-left 8×8 of DCT (lowest frequencies = most image information)
// 5. Compute mean of the 64 values
// 6. For each of 64 values: bit = 1 if value > mean, else 0
// 7. Result: 64-bit pHash
//
// Comparison: Hamming distance between two pHashes
// Distance 0: identical images
// Distance 1-10: near-duplicate (same image, minor modification)
// Distance > 20: different images (false positive risk increases here)
//
// Lookup in database:
// Cannot use exact match (pHash for modified copies will differ)
// Use: bit-parallel Hamming distance computation on database of known hashes
// Or: LSH (locality-sensitive hashing) for approximate nearest neighbor at scale

ML Classifier Design

Different violation types require different model architectures:

  • Image classification (NSFW/violence/hate symbols): fine-tuned CLIP or ResNet/ViT trained on labeled violation examples. Output: confidence score per category. Challenge: CLIP learned internet semantics, may have biases toward certain demographics. Require careful fairness evaluation across demographic groups.
  • Text classification (hate speech/spam): BERT or multilingual XLM-R fine-tuned on labeled text. Challenge: (1) multilingual — must handle 100+ languages, (2) context-dependent — the same words can be hate speech or reclaimed language depending on speaker and context, (3) sarcasm and coded language ("that's so edgy" used to praise harm), (4) adversarial evasion (substituting characters: "h@te sp33ch").
  • Video classification: sample frames at 1-2fps, classify each frame. For audio: transcribe using ASR (Whisper or similar), then text-classify transcript. Video-specific: temporal context (isolated violent frame vs sustained violence), title + description + thumbnail as additional signals.

Human Review Queue Design

The human review queue is a priority queue with SLA constraints. Design considerations:

  • Priority levels: must be fine-grained. CSAM suspected: 1-hour SLA. Terrorism: 4-hour SLA. Graphic violence: 8-hour SLA. Hate speech: 24-hour SLA. Spam: 48-72 hours. Priority is not just violation severity — it's also about potential for ongoing harm (live content > archived, trending content > obscure).
  • Reviewer specialization: some violations require language expertise (hate speech in Arabic requires Arabic-speaking reviewer). Some require legal or cultural expertise (child nudity laws vary by jurisdiction). Route tasks to qualified reviewers, not just the first available reviewer.
  • Consensus for borderline cases: for cases near the boundary (e.g., ML confidence 0.5), route to multiple reviewers (2-3). Require majority agreement. Track inter-reviewer agreement as a quality signal — low agreement indicates ambiguous policies.
  • Reviewer bias mitigation: randomize the order in which content appears to reviewers. Don't show reviewer the previous decision on content they're reviewing. Audit reviewer decisions for systematic bias (are reviewers from country X more likely to remove content from country Y?).
  • Queue fairness: FIFO within each priority level. Don't let a flood of low-priority tasks starve high-priority tasks. Separate queues per priority level, served in priority order.

Adversarial Robustness

Bad actors actively try to evade moderation. This is a cat-and-mouse game:

  • Image evasion: add imperceptible noise to images (adversarial examples), slightly crop known CSAM images, add thin white border, flip horizontally. Counter: pHash is robust to minor modifications; detect at multiple scales; use neural hash (SSCD) that is more robust to adversarial perturbations than pHash.
  • Text evasion: replace letters with similar-looking characters (homoglyphs: "v1agra", "h@te", "k1ll"), insert zero-width characters, use URLs that redirect to harmful content, rely on dog whistles (coded language where community knows meaning but classifier doesn't). Counter: normalization (map homoglyphs to base characters), context window (see surrounding conversation), hash-based detection for known evasion patterns.
  • Video evasion: slightly modify known-bad video (change playback speed, add watermark, invert colors). Counter: temporal analysis (detect even frame-level modifications), audio fingerprinting.
  • Account rotation: get banned → create new account → repeat. Counter: device fingerprinting, IP/phone number reputation, require phone number verification, velocity checks on account creation.
⚠️ Precision vs recall asymmetry: The cost of false positives (removing legitimate content) is different from false negatives (missing a violation) depending on violation type. For CSAM: false negative is catastrophic (legal liability, severe harm) → maximize recall, accept false positives → route borderline cases to human review aggressively. For spam: false positive cost is low (spam poster loses post) and false negative cost is low (spam is annoying but not catastrophic) → higher threshold for automated removal, lower priority for human review. Design must encode this asymmetry explicitly, not assume one policy fits all violation types.

🚀D — Deliver2 min

Summary for the interview:

Content moderation is a three-layer pipeline: (1) hash-based matching for known-bad content (deterministic, fast, no false positives, legally mandated for CSAM) — handles all known violations immediately; (2) ML classifiers for novel violations — fan-out across image/text/video models, route to human review if confidence in uncertain range; (3) human review priority queue — ordered by severity with SLA, specialist routing for language/type. The key architectural tension is proactive vs reactive: CSAM must be proactive (block before visible), spam can be reactive. Violation-type-specific thresholds encode the asymmetric cost of false positive vs false negative for each category.

Phased delivery:

  • Phase 1: Hash matching (CSAM/known violations) + basic ML classifiers + human review queue. Covers legal requirements first.
  • Phase 2: Multi-modal classifiers (image + video + text), priority queue with SLAs, appeals process, audit logging.
  • Phase 3: Adversarial robustness improvements, reviewer quality monitoring, inter-reviewer agreement tracking, transparency reporting dashboard.

⚖️Trade-offs

DecisionOption AOption BRecommended
Proactive vs reactive Proactive: zero visibility window for harmful content, but adds upload latency, can block legitimate content, must match upload throughput Reactive: immediate publish, async moderation, visibility window exists for harmful content before removal Proactive for CSAM (legal mandate) and terrorism (severe harm). Reactive for everything else with priority-based removal timeline.

ML-only: can detect novel violations, but has false positives/negatives, requires retraining, black-box decisions Both layers: hashing for known violations (must run first, can short-circuit ML), ML for novel violations.

Never human-only at scale.
Centralized vs distributed review Centralized review team (one office): consistent policy application, easier training, but single timezone coverage and single-culture bias Distributed global reviewers: 24/7 coverage, local language/culture expertise, but harder to maintain policy consistency Distributed with strong policy training and inter-reviewer agreement auditing. Essential for multilingual content at global scale.

☕Java Implementation

/**
 * ModerationPipeline — orchestrates the multi-layer moderation decision for a content item.
 * Layer 1: Hash matching (synchronous, fast — for CSAM and known violations)
 * Layer 2: ML classification (parallel fan-out across modality-specific classifiers)
 * Layer 3: Route to human review if ML confidence is in uncertain range
 */
public class ModerationPipeline {

    private static final double AUTO_REMOVE_THRESHOLD = 0.95;   // very confident it's a violation
    private static final double AUTO_APPROVE_THRESHOLD = 0.10;  // very confident it's benign

    private final HashMatchDetector hashMatchDetector;
    private final List<ContentClassifier> mlClassifiers;
    private final ReviewQueueService reviewQueueService;
    private final ModerationAuditLog auditLog;
    private final ExecutorService classifierExecutorService;

    public ModerationPipeline(HashMatchDetector hashMatchDetector,
                               List<ContentClassifier> mlClassifiers,
                               ReviewQueueService reviewQueueService,
                               ModerationAuditLog auditLog) {
        this.hashMatchDetector = hashMatchDetector;
        this.mlClassifiers = List.copyOf(mlClassifiers);
        this.reviewQueueService = reviewQueueService;
        this.auditLog = auditLog;
        this.classifierExecutorService = Executors.newFixedThreadPool(
            Runtime.getRuntime().availableProcessors() * 2
        );
    }

    /**
     * Processes a content item through the full moderation pipeline.
     * For CSAM: blocking (synchronous) — content not published until this returns.
     * For other violations: non-blocking (async) — content may be visible during processing.
     *
     * @return ModerationDecision — APPROVED, REMOVED, or PENDING_HUMAN_REVIEW
     */
    public ModerationDecision process(ContentItem contentItem) {
        // Layer 1: Hash matching (always synchronous — must run before publish for CSAM)
        HashMatchResult hashResult = hashMatchDetector.check(contentItem);
        if (hashResult.isMatch()) {
            ModerationDecision decision = ModerationDecision.ofAutomatedRemoval(
                contentItem.contentId(),
                hashResult.violationCategory(),
                1.0,  // perfect confidence for hash match
                "hash_match:" + hashResult.hashType() + ":" + hashResult.matchedHash()
            );
            auditLog.record(decision);

            // CSAM: mandatory legal report to NCMEC
            if (hashResult.violationCategory() == ViolationCategory.CSAM) {
                reportToNcmec(contentItem, hashResult);
            }

            return decision;
        }

        // Layer 2: ML classification (fan-out to all relevant classifiers in parallel)
        List<ClassificationResult> classifierResults = runClassifiersInParallel(contentItem);

        // Aggregate results: take the highest severity finding across all classifiers
        ClassificationResult highestSeverityResult = classifierResults.stream()
            .max(Comparator.comparingDouble(ClassificationResult::confidenceScore))
            .orElse(ClassificationResult.benign());

        double maxConfidence = highestSeverityResult.confidenceScore();

        // Route based on confidence
        if (maxConfidence >= AUTO_REMOVE_THRESHOLD) {
            ModerationDecision decision = ModerationDecision.ofAutomatedRemoval(
                contentItem.contentId(),
                highestSeverityResult.violationCategory(),
                maxConfidence,
                "ml_classifier:" + highestSeverityResult.classifierName()
            );
            auditLog.record(decision);
            return decision;
        }

        if (maxConfidence <= AUTO_APPROVE_THRESHOLD) {
            ModerationDecision decision = ModerationDecision.ofAutomatedApproval(
                contentItem.contentId(), maxConfidence
            );
            auditLog.record(decision);
            return decision;
        }

        // Uncertain range: route to human review queue
        ReviewTask reviewTask = ReviewTask.builder()
            .contentId(contentItem.contentId())
            .priority(calculateReviewPriority(highestSeverityResult.violationCategory()))
            .violationHint(highestSeverityResult.violationCategory())
            .mlConfidence(maxConfidence)
            .localeRequired(contentItem.detectedLanguage())
            .build();

        reviewQueueService.enqueue(reviewTask);
        return ModerationDecision.ofPendingHumanReview(contentItem.contentId());
    }

    /**
     * Runs all ML classifiers in parallel using CompletableFuture.
     * Fans out to image, text, and video classifiers simultaneously.
     * Collects results within a timeout; missing results default to benign.
     */
    private List<ClassificationResult> runClassifiersInParallel(ContentItem contentItem) {
        List<CompletableFuture<ClassificationResult>> classifierFutures = mlClassifiers.stream()
            .filter(classifier -> classifier.canProcess(contentItem.contentType()))
            .map(classifier -> CompletableFuture
                .supplyAsync(() -> classifier.classify(contentItem), classifierExecutorService)
                .orTimeout(5, TimeUnit.SECONDS)  // 5s timeout per classifier
                .exceptionally(classifierError -> {
                    System.err.println("[ModerationPipeline] Classifier " +
                        classifier.name() + " failed: " + classifierError.getMessage());
                    return ClassificationResult.benign();  // Fail open for classifier errors
                }))
            .collect(Collectors.toList());

        return classifierFutures.stream()
            .map(CompletableFuture::join)
            .collect(Collectors.toList());
    }

    private int calculateReviewPriority(ViolationCategory violationCategory) {
        return switch (violationCategory) {
            case CSAM -> 1;
            case TERRORISM, VIOLENT_EXTREMISM -> 2;
            case GRAPHIC_VIOLENCE, CREDIBLE_THREAT -> 3;
            case HATE_SPEECH, SEVERE_HARASSMENT -> 4;
            case SPAM, MISINFORMATION, COPYRIGHT -> 5;
            default -> 5;
        };
    }

    private void reportToNcmec(ContentItem contentItem, HashMatchResult hashResult) {
        // NCMEC CyberTipline API integration - legally required for CSAM
        System.out.println("[ModerationPipeline] Submitting CSAM report to NCMEC for content: " +
            contentItem.contentId());
        // Implementation: call NCMEC CyberTipline API with content details and uploader info
    }
}

// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// HashMatchDetector — checks content against hash databases for known violations.
// Uses SHA-256 for exact match and pHash for near-duplicate detection.
// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
public class HashMatchDetector {

    private final Set<String> exactHashDatabase;           // SHA-256 hashes (in memory)
    private final Map<Long, ViolationCategory> pHashDatabase;  // pHash long values (in memory)
    private static final int PHASH_HAMMING_THRESHOLD = 10;  // max bit difference for "same image"

    public HashMatchDetector(Set<String> exactHashDatabase,
                              Map<Long, ViolationCategory> pHashDatabase) {
        this.exactHashDatabase = Set.copyOf(exactHashDatabase);
        this.pHashDatabase = Map.copyOf(pHashDatabase);
    }

    public HashMatchResult check(ContentItem contentItem) {
        if (contentItem.contentType() != ContentType.IMAGE
                && contentItem.contentType() != ContentType.VIDEO) {
            return HashMatchResult.noMatch();
        }

        byte[] contentBytes = loadContentBytes(contentItem.storageUri());

        // Check 1: Exact SHA-256 hash
        String sha256Hash = computeSha256(contentBytes);
        if (exactHashDatabase.contains(sha256Hash)) {
            return HashMatchResult.match(
                HashType.EXACT_SHA256,
                sha256Hash,
                ViolationCategory.CSAM  // Exact hashes are primarily CSAM
            );
        }

        // Check 2: Perceptual hash (near-duplicate)
        long pHashValue = computePerceptualHash(contentBytes);
        for (Map.Entry<Long, ViolationCategory> entry : pHashDatabase.entrySet()) {
            int hammingDistance = Long.bitCount(pHashValue ^ entry.getKey());
            if (hammingDistance <= PHASH_HAMMING_THRESHOLD) {
                return HashMatchResult.match(
                    HashType.PERCEPTUAL_PHASH,
                    String.valueOf(entry.getKey()),
                    entry.getValue()
                );
            }
        }

        return HashMatchResult.noMatch();
    }

    private String computeSha256(byte[] content) {
        try {

            byte[] hashBytes = digest.digest(content);
            return HexFormat.of().formatHex(hashBytes);
        } catch (NoSuchAlgorithmException impossible) {
            throw new RuntimeException("SHA-256 not available", impossible);
        }
    }

// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// PriorityReviewQueue — manages the human review task queue with SLA enforcement.
// Uses PriorityBlockingQueue sorted by (priority, enqueuedAt) for fairness.
// ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
public class PriorityReviewQueue {

    // Priority: lower number = higher priority (1=CSAM, 5=spam)
    private final PriorityBlockingQueue<ReviewTask> queue = new PriorityBlockingQueue<>(
        1000,
        Comparator.comparingInt(ReviewTask::priority)
                  .thenComparing(ReviewTask::enqueuedAt)  // FIFO within same priority
    );

    private static final Map<Integer, Duration> SLA_BY_PRIORITY = Map.of(
        1, Duration.ofHours(1),    // CSAM
        2, Duration.ofHours(4),    // Terrorism
        3, Duration.ofHours(8),    // Graphic violence
        4, Duration.ofHours(24),   // Hate speech
        5, Duration.ofHours(72)    // Spam/copyright
    );

    public void enqueue(ReviewTask task) {
        Duration slaDuration = SLA_BY_PRIORITY.getOrDefault(task.priority(), Duration.ofHours(72));
        ReviewTask taskWithSla = task.withDueBy(Instant.now().plus(slaDuration));
        queue.offer(taskWithSla);
    }

    /**
     * Assigns the next task to a reviewer.
     * Filters by locale if reviewer has language expertise.
     * Returns Optional.empty() if no suitable task available.
     */
    public Optional<ReviewTask> assignNextTask(String reviewerId, String reviewerLocale) {
        // Drain queue to find a task matching reviewer's language capability
        List<ReviewTask> skippedTasks = new ArrayList<>();
        Optional<ReviewTask> assignedTask = Optional.empty();

        while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
            ReviewTask candidate = queue.poll();
            if (candidate == null) break;

            boolean localeMatch = candidate.localeRequired() == null
                || candidate.localeRequired().equals(reviewerLocale)
                || reviewerLocale.equals("any");  // some reviewers handle all locales

            if (localeMatch) {
                assignedTask = Optional.of(candidate.withAssignee(reviewerId, Instant.now()));
                break;
            } else {
                skippedTasks.add(candidate);
            }
        }

        // Re-enqueue skipped tasks (they'll be picked up by appropriate reviewers)
        queue.addAll(skippedTasks);
        return assignedTask;
    }

    public int queueDepth() { return queue.size(); }

    public long slaBreachCount() {
        return queue.stream()
            .filter(task -> task.dueBy() != null && task.dueBy().isBefore(Instant.now()))
            .count();
    }
}

📚Advanced Moderation Patterns

Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior (CIB) Detection

Beyond individual content violations, platforms must detect coordinated campaigns — networks of accounts working together to amplify harmful content, spread misinformation, or manipulate metrics. This requires graph-level analysis, not per-content analysis:

  • Account clustering: accounts that consistently interact with each other, post similar content, were created in the same time window, share device fingerprints, or use the same IP range. Graph clustering algorithms (Louvain community detection) identify suspicious clusters.
  • Velocity signals: a piece of content that goes from 0 to 1M shares in 2 hours is either genuinely viral or coordinated amplification. Distinguish via engagement quality metrics: are shares from diverse accounts or from a cluster? Are shares accompanied by comments or are they pure reshares (bot behavior)?
  • Cross-platform signals: the same content or campaign appearing across Twitter, Facebook, YouTube simultaneously suggests coordination. Platforms share threat intelligence via initiatives like GIFCT (Global Internet Forum to Counter Terrorism).

Content Moderation Policy as Code

Policy rules must be explicit, auditable, and testable — not just embedded in model weights. Production systems encode policy as structured rules evaluated alongside ML predictions:

// Policy rules (simplified — real systems have hundreds of these)
// Rules are evaluated in order; first matching rule wins
//
// Rule 1: CSAM — unconditional block, always report
// IF content_type IN (IMAGE, VIDEO)
//   AND hash_match_type = PHOTODNA
// THEN action = BLOCK_AND_REPORT, mandatory_report = NCMEC
//
// Rule 2: Terrorism propaganda — high-confidence auto-remove
// IF ml_score.terrorism > 0.95
//   AND content_length_seconds > 60  // not a short clip showing news context
// THEN action = REMOVE, notify_user = true
//
// Rule 3: Graphic violence — route to human review
// IF ml_score.graphic_violence > 0.70
//   AND content_type = VIDEO
//   AND uploader_account_age_days < 30  // new accounts are higher risk
// THEN action = ROUTE_HUMAN_REVIEW, priority = 3
//
// Rule 4: News exception — reduce false positives for news content
// IF ml_score.graphic_violence > 0.70
//   AND uploader_is_verified_news_organization = true
// THEN action = APPROVE_WITH_WARNING_LABEL  // show but label as potentially disturbing
//
// Policy as code benefits:
// - Auditable: every decision can be traced to a specific rule
// - Testable: regression tests ensure policy changes don't break existing cases
// - Explainable: reviewer and user see which rule triggered the decision
// - Versioned: policy history tracked in Git like source code

Reviewer Tool Design for Accuracy and Throughput

Human reviewers handle millions of decisions per day at Facebook scale. Reviewer tool design directly impacts both accuracy and reviewer wellbeing:

  • Context display: show reviewer the flagged content plus context: the conversation thread it appeared in, the account's history, the account's previous violation decisions. Context reduces false positives dramatically for borderline hate speech (same words mean different things in different contexts).
  • Structured decision forms: rather than free-text, reviewers select from a structured taxonomy (violation category → subcategory → specific policy rule). This produces machine-readable audit trails and enables systematic policy improvement.
  • Quality calibration: periodically inject "gold standard" cases (cases with known correct answers) into the reviewer queue. Track each reviewer's accuracy on gold cases. Reviewers with low accuracy get additional training or are flagged for quality review. This ensures consistent policy enforcement across thousands of reviewers globally.
  • Workflow efficiency: keyboard shortcuts for common decisions. Batch review of similar content (all reported content from the same account in one session). Pre-load next item while reviewer reads current item. Small UX improvements compound over millions of reviews.

Crisis Response: Handling Coordinated Attacks

Real-world events (terrorist attacks, elections, breaking news) trigger coordinated mass uploads of violating content. The moderation system must handle 100x normal violation volume in minutes:

  • Hash bank rapid expansion: when the Christchurch attack video was uploaded in 2019, platforms needed to add its hash to the detection database within minutes and have that propagate to all edge nodes. The hash database update path must support emergency hot deployment.
  • Human review surge capacity: redirect reviewers from lower-priority queues (spam, copyright) to high-priority crisis queues (terrorism). Cross-train reviewers so they can serve multiple violation categories.
  • Auto-escalation from reactive to proactive: normally, new accounts are reactive. During a coordinated campaign, temporarily flip to proactive for all new video uploads in the affected content category until the crisis is contained.
  • Cross-platform emergency protocols: GIFCT's Hash-Sharing Database allows platforms to share hashes of terrorist content within 2 hours of a major attack. Platforms subscribe to this database feed and ingest hashes automatically.
